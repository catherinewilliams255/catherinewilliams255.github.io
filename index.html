Unstable Archives
In 1969, sculptor Forrest Myers orchestrated the placement of the first artwork on the Moon. The project, entitled Moon Museum, consists of drawings by six artists, including Robert Rauschenberg, Forrest Myers, and Andy Warhol, miniaturized and printed onto a tiny ceramic wafer surreptitiously attached to the lunar module of NASAss Apollo 12 mission. It remains on the lunar surface to this day.

It's a powerful statement about permanence. While art on Earth is subject to decay, theft, and human intervention, the Moon Museum is likely to remain untouched for millions, if not billions, of years.
It is, quite literally, a timeless piece. A silent testament to human ingenuity long after our terrestrial civilisations may have faded.
It reflects a belief in legacy as permanence, where art becomes an unchanging marker of human existence.

01

Presence

The original Moon Museum was a quiet assertion of presence: six artists placing a trace of human creativity beyond Earth. It was small, basically invisible, yet carried the weight of saying: we were here.

The Earth is intimate and expressive. It is expressed by billions of stories and 'marks'.

It's also a powerful statement about permanence. While art on Earth is subject to decay, theft, and human intervention, the Moon Museum is likely to remain untouched for millions, if not billions, of years.

1. The footprints the Apollo astronauts left on the Moon in 1969. Physical evidence mankind stopped being confined to one world.
02

Legacy

Legacy is often understood as what is left behind; something intended to endure beyond a single lifetime, carrying memory, or value into the future. It implies preservation and a sense of completion. The microchip on the Moon suggested that art could outlast humanity itself. It becomes less about permanence as authority, and more about permanence as desire.

From NASA's perspective, any unauthorized item represented a potential risk and a breach of protocol. From the artists' and engineers' perspective, it was a necessary act to achieve a higher purpose, to broaden the definition of human achievement in space.

Legacy is no longer guaranteed to endure unchanged; instead, it exists within conditions of instability, where things can fade, distort, or disappear entirely. Legacy is not reframed as something that must last forever, but as the act of leaving a trace at all. It becomes an expression of presence and intention, even if what remains is partial or eventually lost.

2. A photograph of astronaut Charles Duke's family that was left behind on the lunar surface.
03

Archive

The Museum functioned as a micro-archive, it was selective and closed. It reflected a narrow slice of cultural production at the time. Today, archives are vast and decentralised, and include personal data, collective memory, overlooked and marginalised voices.

In todays reality, art can be open and participatory, incomplete and evolving, and resistant to a single narrative. It fosters questions and develops projects that lay the foundation for a future interplanetary humanity. Through these initiatives, the foundation brings together artists, scientists, agencies, and industry to exchange knowledge and develop sustainable cultural practices.

3. The Moon Gallery; a pioneering international art installation launched to the International Space Station.
04

Echo

What survives and what is lost in the act of remembering?

Performative, and deeply entwined with personal branding and spectacle. Unlike earlier gestures such as the Moon Museum, which quietly embedded art into a larger mission, the Roadster was intentionally visible, ironic, and absure, a luxury consumer object launched into orbit, accompanied by a mannequin and David Bowie playing into the void. Culturally, it blurred the boundaries between engineering and performance, suggesting that space is no longer just a frontier of discovery but also a stage for humour and ego. It reflected a shift toward a privatised cosmos, where individuals and companies can project identity beyond Earth, raising questions about authorship, ownership, and who gets to represent humanity in space. Widely read as a gesture about excess, ambition, and the human desire to leave a mark, even in the most impractical ways. It resonates with contemporary practices that embrace spectacle, irony, and critique, while also exposing tensions between sustainability and visibility, meaning and novelty. Like many current artworks, it exists somewhere between sincerity and satire, asking whether it is a meaningful cultural artefact or simply an image circulating through media.
The object continues to exist in space, but what we experience on Earth is only its afterimage, a repeated and mediated trace that shifts with time. Like an echo, it is no longer the original signal. It is a version altered by distance and interpretation.

05

Signal

Human-filled with messages, confessions, and jokes. Imperfection is not corrected but preserved, where human presence is felt through flaws: misspellings, half-finished drawings, and shaky, uncertain lines. These fragments carry a sense of immediacy, revealing not polished outcomes but moments in progress.

Since so much of life now exists online, the museum would also reflect a digital visual language, compressed images, pixelated icons, fragments of social feeds, and code-like structures or data imprints. Together, these elements create a tension between the immaterial world we inhabit and the persistent desire to make something that lasts.

06

Transmission

The Apollo 12 Moon Museum wasn't an isolated incident. It was part of a broader, though often understated, desire to integrate art and culture with humanity's ventures into space. It serves as an early, pivotal example in a growing tradition. The original Moon Museum collapsed transmission into placement, the journey was invisible, and the outcome was fixed.

Transmission becomes less about delivery and more about vulnerability: an acknowledgement that every act of sending carries with it the possibility of failure, transformation, and misreading. In this way, the Moon Museum becomes more of a continuous act of reaching outward, where meaning exists the unstable space in between.

Since its creation, the landscape of space exploration has shifted significantly. It is no longer controlled solely by governments, with private companies like SpaceX commercialising access and reshaping its purpose. There is also a growing awareness of inclusivity and representation in global narratives, challenging who gets to speak for humanity. At the same time, the once unbridled optimism surrounding space exploration is now tempered by concerns about space debris, colonisation, and the ethics of extending human activity beyond Earth.
